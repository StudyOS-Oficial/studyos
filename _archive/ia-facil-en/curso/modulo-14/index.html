BONUS MODULE 4 · 10 LESSONS

Work with images, files and voice

Go beyond text. Learn how to use AI with photos, screenshots, PDFs, documents and voice while understanding what AI can and cannot reliably interpret.

LESSON 1

AI can understand more than text

Modern AI tools can work with images and many types of files, not only written prompts.

In depth: ChatGPT can accept image inputs and can work with documents, spreadsheets, presentations and supported audio files. This changes the way you can ask for help: instead of describing everything yourself, you can sometimes provide the original material and ask AI to analyse it. Availability and limits depend on the plan, model, platform and account, so a feature you see in one context may not appear in another.
Try it: Take a non-sensitive photo of something you want help understanding and ask: “What can you identify here, and what are you uncertain about?”
LESSON 2

Ask questions about a photo

A photo can provide useful context for practical questions, from identifying objects to understanding what is visible.

In depth: Image input is useful when the visual information itself matters. You can ask about objects, visible text, layout, diagrams or other visual details. Be specific about the part you want analysed. If the image is blurry, poorly lit or ambiguous, the answer may be less reliable. Ask AI to state uncertainty instead of assuming that a confident description is necessarily correct.
Try it: Upload a photo of a household object and ask: “Describe what you can see. Which details are clear and which are uncertain?”
LESSON 3

Use screenshots to get help

A screenshot can show AI exactly what you are seeing instead of forcing you to describe every button or message.

In depth: Screenshots are especially useful for troubleshooting, explaining interfaces and understanding error messages. Crop or mark the relevant area when the screen contains distracting or private information. Ask one concrete question at a time. If the answer depends on a setting that is not visible, AI should tell you what additional information is needed rather than inventing it.
Try it: Upload a harmless screenshot and ask: “What does this message mean, and what should I check next?”
LESSON 4

Work with PDFs and documents

You can upload supported documents and ask AI to summarise, explain, compare or extract information from them.

In depth: File analysis is useful for turning long material into questions and structured information. You can ask for a summary, a list of key points, a comparison between documents or an explanation of a difficult section. For important work, check the result against the original because extraction and interpretation can contain errors. Ask AI to point to the relevant section when accuracy matters.
Try it: Upload a non-sensitive PDF and ask: “Give me five key points, then tell me which page or section supports each one.”
LESSON 5

Turn a document into something useful

AI can transform information from a file into a checklist, table, summary, study guide or draft.

In depth: Transformation is often more useful than simple summarisation. Tell AI what the output is for, who will use it and what must be preserved. If the source contains important wording, numbers or conditions, explicitly ask AI not to omit them. Treat the transformed version as a working aid and keep the original available for verification.
Try it: Ask: “Turn this document into a one-page checklist. Keep all deadlines and requirements exactly as written.”
LESSON 6

Use spreadsheets without losing control

AI can help understand tables and organise data, but important numbers should still be checked.

In depth: Spreadsheet analysis can help identify patterns, summarise columns, explain formulas or organise information. Before sharing a file, remove data that AI does not need. When numbers affect money, work or important decisions, inspect the source data and calculations yourself. Ask AI to explain its method so you can see what it used and what assumptions it made.
Try it: With a simple practice spreadsheet, ask: “Summarise the main patterns and show me how you reached each conclusion.”
LESSON 7

Talk instead of typing

Voice can make AI easier to use when speaking is more natural than writing.

In depth: Voice conversations can be useful for brainstorming, asking quick questions or practising explanations. Availability and capabilities vary by account and platform. Spoken conversations can also create misunderstandings, especially with names, numbers or accents. For important information, slow down, confirm key details and switch to text when you need a precise record.
Try it: Ask a simple question by voice, then ask AI to repeat the key details in a short written list.
LESSON 8

Give AI the right material

The quality of the input matters. Good results usually begin with a clear file, image or recording and a specific goal.

In depth: Before uploading anything, ask yourself: What does AI actually need? Remove irrelevant pages, private information and unnecessary personal details when possible. Tell AI what to focus on and what output you want. If the material is incomplete, say so. AI should work from the evidence you provide rather than filling missing information with guesses.
Try it: Use this pattern: “Here is [material]. Focus on [specific part]. I want [specific output]. Tell me what you cannot determine.”
LESSON 9

Know the limits of visual and file analysis

AI can misunderstand images, extract information incorrectly or miss important details.

In depth: Image and file analysis is powerful but not infallible. Blurry images, ambiguous layouts, missing context and complex material can reduce accuracy. Some domains require specialist interpretation and should not be delegated to AI. For high-stakes medical, legal, financial or safety decisions, use qualified sources or professionals and treat AI as an assistant for questions and organisation rather than as the final authority.
Try it: Ask: “What parts of your answer are uncertain, and what should I verify in the original source?”
LESSON 10

Build a multimodal habit

Choose the input that contains the information you need: text, image, file or voice. Then ask for a result you can check.

In depth: The most useful habit is not learning a particular button. It is recognising which form of information will make the task easier. Use a photo when the visual details matter, a document when the source material matters, a spreadsheet when the numbers matter and voice when speaking is more convenient. Keep the human review step, especially when an error could have meaningful consequences.
Try it: Choose one low-risk task this week and complete it using an image, file or voice input. Note what was easier and what still needed your judgement.
