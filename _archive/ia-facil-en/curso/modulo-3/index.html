MODULE 03 · 10 LESSONS

Ask better questions

Use a simple prompting framework to get clearer, more relevant and more controllable results without needing technical language.

LESSON 1

The anatomy of a good prompt

A useful prompt usually contains four ingredients: task, context, constraints and output format. You do not need all four every time, but they give you a reliable starting structure.

In depth: A strong prompt usually contains four building blocks: the task, the context, the desired output and the constraints. You do not need to write these as formal headings, but thinking in this structure prevents important details from being omitted. Good prompting is less about clever phrases and more about communicating the job clearly.
Practical idea: A simple formula is: 'Help me [TASK]. Here is the context: [CONTEXT]. Please respect [CONSTRAINTS]. Return the result as [FORMAT].'
LESSON 2

Describe the task precisely

Replace vague verbs such as 'help', 'do' or 'make' with an observable result: summarise, compare, rewrite, explain, classify, plan, brainstorm or evaluate.

In depth: Precision means defining the action rather than merely naming the topic. “Tell me about Spain” leaves almost everything open, while “Explain three major causes of the Spanish Civil War for a beginner in 250 words” defines the job. Precision means removing ambiguity so the model has fewer important things to guess.
Practical idea: Instead of 'Help with my CV', try 'Rewrite my professional summary for a customer-service role. Keep it under 80 words and use a confident but natural tone.'
LESSON 3

Explain the context

Context tells AI why the task exists. Include the facts that change the answer and leave out irrelevant personal information.

In depth: Context tells the model why the task exists and what situation surrounds it. Relevant context might include your audience, experience, previous attempts, available information and limitations. Good context is selective: include what affects the task and leave out unnecessary or sensitive details.
Practical idea: For a message to a landlord, context might include what happened, when it happened and what solution you want. You do not need to provide unrelated details.
LESSON 4

Set boundaries

Constraints prevent an otherwise useful answer from becoming unrealistic. State limits such as time, money, length, equipment, reading level or number of options.

In depth: Boundaries define what the answer should and should not do. They can cover length, budget, deadline, sources, tone, language or things you do not want included. Boundaries act as guardrails and often prevent predictable problems before they appear in the answer.
Practical idea: Try: 'Give me five ideas, not ten. They must be suitable for a small kitchen and require no specialist equipment.'
LESSON 5

Specify the output

If you know how you want to use the answer, ask for that format directly. A table may be better for comparison; numbered steps may be better for a procedure.

In depth: The output specification describes what the finished answer should look like. You can request a checklist, table, numbered process, short explanation, comparison or draft message. You can also specify approximate length and audience, making the result easier to use without heavy editing.
Practical idea: Prompt: 'Compare these three options in a table with columns for cost, advantages, disadvantages and who each option suits best.'
LESSON 6

Give examples when style matters

If the exact tone or structure matters, show a short example. One good example can communicate your preference more clearly than a paragraph of instructions.

In depth: Examples act as concrete references for what you mean. If you want a particular tone, structure or level of detail, showing one good example can be more precise than a long description. Examples are especially useful for recurring work because they create a stable target for future prompts.
Practical idea: You can say: 'Use a tone like this: friendly, direct and calm. Avoid exaggerated marketing language.' Never assume the AI knows your preferred style without telling it.
LESSON 7

Ask for alternatives

A single answer can make you accept the first reasonable idea. Asking for alternatives exposes trade-offs and gives you choice.

In depth: Alternatives are useful when there is no single obviously correct answer. Define the dimension that should vary: cheapest, fastest, simplest, most formal or most creative. This turns a vague request for “more ideas” into a useful comparison between different strategies.
Practical idea: Try: 'Give me three versions: safest, cheapest and simplest. Explain the main trade-off of each.' This is especially useful for planning and decisions.
LESSON 8

Ask for a critique

AI can review its own draft, but you should treat the critique as assistance rather than proof. Ask it to look for missing assumptions, ambiguity, weak reasoning or unclear language.

In depth: A critique prompt changes the task from generation to evaluation. Ask AI to identify weaknesses, missing information, contradictions or unclear sections before asking for a final rewrite. Give it explicit criteria so the review has a standard rather than becoming a vague opinion.
Practical idea: Prompt: 'Review your previous answer. List three weaknesses and then provide a corrected version.' You can also ask a human to review important work.
LESSON 9

Iterate systematically

Do not change ten instructions at once if you are trying to understand what improves the result. Change one important variable, compare the output and keep what works.

In depth: Iteration works best when you change one or two variables at a time. Inspect the output, identify the main defect, make a targeted change and compare again. This makes prompting more predictable and helps you learn which instructions reliably improve the result.
Practical idea: A useful habit is versioning: Prompt 1 = basic request; Prompt 2 = add audience; Prompt 3 = add constraints. You will quickly learn which details matter.
LESSON 10

Final practical challenge

Take a vague prompt you might normally write, such as 'Plan my holiday', and transform it using task, context, constraints and output. Then ask ChatGPT for the result.

In depth: Create two prompts for a task you know well: a vague version and a structured version containing task, context, constraints and output. Compare the responses and then refine the better prompt once. The exercise demonstrates that effective prompting is mainly clear communication.
Practical idea: Finally, remove one piece of information and run it again. Compare the answers. This experiment teaches you why prompting is really about communication, not magic words.
Do it now: Apply the technique to one real situation from your own life. Keep the task simple enough to finish in 10–15 minutes.
