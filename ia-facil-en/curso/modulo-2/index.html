MODULE 02 · 10 LESSONS

Your first conversation with ChatGPT

Learn how a good conversation works: make a request, provide context, inspect the answer, ask follow-up questions and gradually reach the result you actually need.

LESSON 1

Start with a clear goal

Before typing, ask yourself: 'What do I want at the end?' A goal such as 'write a friendly message to my neighbour about the noise' is more useful than simply saying 'write something'.

In depth: A prompt is easier to answer when the desired outcome is explicit. Compare a vague request such as “help me with my trip” with a defined outcome such as “create a four-day itinerary with train travel, moderate walking and a mid-range budget.” The second request gives the model something concrete to optimise for. A clear goal also makes evaluation easier: once the answer arrives, you can ask whether it actually achieved the intended result.
Practical idea: If the goal is unclear, ask AI to help define it: 'I know I need to organise my finances, but I do not know where to start. Ask me five questions and then suggest a simple first step.'
LESSON 2

Tell it who the answer is for

The same information can need a completely different explanation depending on the audience. Tell ChatGPT whether you are writing for yourself, a child, a customer, a teacher, a colleague or a friend.

In depth: Audience changes vocabulary, depth, examples and tone. An explanation for a child should not be written like a technical manual, and a message to a close friend should not sound like a formal complaint. Telling AI who will read the result removes an important ambiguity. You can also describe the reader's starting level: beginner, experienced user, specialist, customer or student. Audience is therefore not decoration; it is part of the specification of the task.
Practical idea: Try: 'Explain this technical paragraph to someone with no background in the subject.' Then ask for a second version aimed at an expert. Notice how the vocabulary and depth change.
LESSON 3

Give constraints

Constraints make an answer practical. You can specify length, budget, time, tone, ingredients, available equipment, language or anything else that matters.

In depth: Constraints are boundaries that make an answer usable. They can include time, budget, length, available ingredients, equipment, language, location, tone or things you do not want included. Without constraints, the model has to choose them for you, which can lead to an answer that is reasonable but impractical. Think of constraints as guardrails: they narrow the space of possible answers while leaving the model enough freedom to generate useful options.
Practical idea: Example: 'Give me three dinner ideas for two people. Each should take less than 25 minutes, use ordinary supermarket ingredients and cost as little as possible.'
LESSON 4

Ask one task at a time when needed

AI can handle complex requests, but beginners often get better results by separating a large job into stages. First ask for a plan, then ask it to execute each part.

In depth: Complex requests can work, but beginners often gain control by separating a large task into stages. Planning before writing is a good example: first establish the structure, then expand the sections, then review the result. Staging makes mistakes easier to locate and lets you approve one part before spending effort on the next. It also prevents an overloaded prompt from producing a shallow answer that tries to satisfy too many competing instructions at once.
Practical idea: For a presentation, try: 'First give me a five-slide structure.' After reviewing it, say: 'Now write slide 2.' This gives you more control than requesting an entire presentation at once.
LESSON 5

Use follow-up questions

A conversation is valuable because you do not have to repeat everything. Once the relevant context is established, you can refine the result through follow-ups.

In depth: Conversation is one of the main advantages of a conversational AI. Once the relevant context is established, you can refine the output without repeating every detail. Follow-up questions can request alternatives, explanations, corrections, shorter versions or additional examples. Good follow-ups are specific: “make it warmer” tells the model what dimension to change, while “make it better” leaves the meaning of better undefined. Treat each turn as a small adjustment toward the result you want.
Practical idea: Ask: 'What would you change?', 'Can you make it warmer?', 'What am I missing?', or 'Give me an alternative for someone with less time.' Follow-ups turn a generic answer into a personalised one.
LESSON 6

Correct misunderstandings

If ChatGPT misunderstands you, do not assume the whole conversation is ruined. State the correction explicitly and continue.

In depth: When AI misunderstands a detail, identify the exact misunderstanding and preserve the parts that were correct. For example, say what should change and what should stay the same. This is more efficient than assuming you need to start again. You can also ask the model to restate the revised understanding before continuing when the task is important. Clear correction helps prevent a small misunderstanding from propagating through the rest of a long answer.
Practical idea: For example: 'I meant a weekend trip by train, not a day trip by car. Keep the same budget and change only the transport and itinerary.' Clear corrections save time.
LESSON 7

Ask AI to ask you questions

Sometimes you do not know which details matter. Reverse the process: ask ChatGPT what information it needs before producing the answer.

In depth: Sometimes the difficulty is not writing the prompt but knowing what information matters. In that situation, reverse the process and ask AI to interview you first. For a recommendation, it can identify your priorities; for a trip, it can ask about dates, budget and mobility; for a learning task, it can assess your level. This technique turns hidden requirements into explicit information and can dramatically improve the relevance of the final answer.
Practical idea: Prompt: 'Before recommending a laptop, ask me the seven questions you need to know about how I will use it.' Answer the questions, then request the recommendation.
LESSON 8

Separate ideas from facts

AI is particularly useful for generating options and drafts. It is more risky when a response contains factual claims you need to rely on. Tell it which kind of task you want.

In depth: Generative AI is excellent at producing possibilities, drafts and variations because those tasks do not require every statement to be a verified fact. The situation changes when you need factual accuracy. A useful habit is to tell the model which job you want: brainstorming, explanation, analysis or factual research. For factual claims that matter, identify what needs verification and use reliable sources rather than treating fluent language as evidence.
Practical idea: For brainstorming: 'Give me 15 possible names.' For factual work: 'List the claims that need verification and tell me what source type I should check.'
LESSON 9

Know when to start a new chat

A long conversation can accumulate context that is no longer relevant. Starting a new chat can make a task cleaner, especially when switching from one subject to another.

In depth: Conversation history is useful when earlier information remains relevant, but it can become a source of confusion when the subject changes completely. A new chat creates a cleaner context. You can still carry over only the information that matters by summarising it or pasting the relevant part. There is no universal rule for when to start fresh: the practical question is whether the previous conversation helps the current task or introduces assumptions that could distort the answer.
Practical idea: Keep a conversation when the history helps. Start fresh when the old context could confuse the new task. You can always paste the small amount of information that matters.
LESSON 10

Final practical challenge

Have a five-minute conversation with ChatGPT about a real task. First state the goal. Then add two constraints. Ask for a first answer, critique it, and request a revised version.

In depth: Build one complete conversation around a real, low-risk task. Start by stating the goal, then add audience and constraints. Read the answer critically and ask at least two follow-up questions that improve it. Finally, identify one claim or recommendation you would verify before acting. You are practising the full conversation loop: goal, context, output, inspection and refinement.
Practical idea: At the end, compare the first and final answers. Identify the single instruction that made the biggest difference. That is the beginning of your personal prompting style.
Do it now: Apply the technique to one real situation from your own life. Keep the task simple enough to finish in 10–15 minutes.
FINAL CHECK

Put the module into practice

Before moving on, answer these questions for yourself:

  1. What is the most useful idea from this module?
  2. Where could you apply it this week?
  3. What would you need to verify before trusting the result?

Remember: AI is most useful when you stay in control, provide good instructions and review what comes back.
