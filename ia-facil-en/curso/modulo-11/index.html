MODULE 11 · 10 LESSONS · BONUS

Beyond chat · Bonus

Go beyond simple questions and learn how to use AI as a flexible thinking partner: give it roles, constraints, examples and a process so it can help with more complex tasks.

LESSON 1

Think in stages

A complex task is easier when you separate it into steps: understand the goal, gather information, create options, review them and produce the final result.

In depth: Complex work becomes easier to control when you divide it into stages. First clarify the goal, then plan, then generate, then review. Each stage gives you a checkpoint where you can correct assumptions before they spread through the rest of the task.
Try it: Ask: 'First help me define the problem. Then propose three approaches. Do not write the final answer until I choose one.'
LESSON 2

Give AI a role

A useful role gives the conversation a point of view, such as teacher, editor, coach or project planner. The role does not make the answer automatically correct.

In depth: A role gives the model a useful perspective for the task, such as tutor, editor, planner or interviewer. It does not make the model a real professional. Combine the role with the task, audience and desired result so the instruction has practical meaning.
Try it: Try: 'Act as a patient tutor. Explain this concept to a beginner, then ask me two questions to check my understanding.'
LESSON 3

Set clear constraints

Tell AI what must be included, what must be avoided, the length, audience and format. Constraints reduce vague results.

In depth: Constraints reduce ambiguity. State limits such as length, time, budget, tone, language or required elements. When a constraint is important, make it explicit instead of expecting AI to infer it from the situation.
Try it: Try: 'Write this in 150 words, for a beginner, with three examples and no technical jargon.'
LESSON 4

Use examples

Showing AI an example of the kind of result you want can be more useful than adding lots of abstract instructions.

In depth: A concrete example can communicate structure or style more precisely than abstract instructions. Give a good example and explain what characteristics matter. Examples are particularly powerful when you are creating something repeatedly.
Try it: Give one good example and say which features should be preserved.
LESSON 5

Ask for alternatives

Instead of accepting the first answer, ask for different approaches and compare their advantages and disadvantages.

In depth: Alternatives are valuable when several reasonable solutions exist. Tell AI which dimension should change—cost, speed, simplicity, tone or creativity—so the options become meaningfully different rather than minor variations of the same answer.
Try it: Try: 'Give me three versions: simplest, most detailed and most practical. Explain when each is best.'
LESSON 6

Ask AI to critique its draft

A second pass can expose unclear sections, missing assumptions or weak arguments. Treat the critique as a review, not as proof of correctness.

In depth: A critique step separates creation from evaluation. Ask AI to inspect its own draft for missing requirements, unclear wording, contradictions or unsupported claims. Treat the critique as a useful second opinion, not as proof that the final result is correct.
Try it: Ask: 'Critique this draft. List the three biggest weaknesses and suggest specific improvements.'
LESSON 7

Build reusable prompt patterns

When a task repeats, save a prompt structure with placeholders for the changing information.

In depth: A reusable prompt pattern captures instructions that work repeatedly while leaving changing details as placeholders. This turns successful conversations into a small personal library and reduces the effort needed to start familiar tasks.
Try it: Create a template such as: goal + context + constraints + output format + checks.
LESSON 8

Use conversations strategically

Long conversations can accumulate context, but they can also become confusing. Periodically ask for a concise summary of decisions and open questions.

In depth: Conversation history is useful when previous context remains relevant. Use follow-up messages to refine the result rather than repeating everything. When the subject changes, consider starting fresh so old assumptions do not contaminate the new task.
Try it: Try: 'Summarise what we have decided so far and list what is still unresolved.'
LESSON 9

Know when to start a new chat

A fresh conversation can be better when the subject changes completely or old context could bias the result.

In depth: A new chat is useful when the old context is no longer relevant or has become confusing. Keep the previous conversation when its history genuinely helps. If you start fresh, carry over only the information needed for the new task.
Try it: If you change from planning a holiday to writing a formal complaint, starting a new chat can keep the context clean.
LESSON 10

Final challenge

Choose a real low-risk task and use a staged workflow: define the goal, set constraints, ask for options, critique the chosen approach and produce the final result.

In depth: Choose a task that normally requires several steps. Divide it into stages, define a role and constraints, request alternatives and add a critique step. Run the process once and note which stage gave you the most useful improvement.
Try it: Finish with your own review. The objective is to control the process rather than simply ask one question.
