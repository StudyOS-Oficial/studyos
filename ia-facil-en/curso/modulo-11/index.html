BONUS MODULE 1 · 10 LESSONS

Beyond chat · Bonus 1

Go beyond simple questions and learn how to use AI as a flexible thinking partner: give it roles, constraints, examples and a process so it can help with more complex tasks.

LESSON 1

Think in stages

A complex task is easier when you separate it into steps: understand the goal, gather information, create options, review them and produce the final result.

In depth: Complex work becomes easier to control when you divide it into stages. First clarify the goal, then plan, then generate, then review. Each stage gives you a checkpoint where you can correct assumptions before they spread through the rest of the task. Name a deliverable for each stage, such as an outline, options or a checked draft. Clear checkpoints help you notice when the work has drifted before you spend effort on later steps.
Try it: Ask: 'First help me define the problem. Then propose three approaches. Do not write the final answer until I choose one.'
LESSON 2

Give AI a role

A useful role gives the conversation a point of view, such as teacher, editor, coach or project planner. The role does not make the answer automatically correct.

In depth: A role gives the model a useful perspective for the task, such as tutor, editor, planner or interviewer. It does not make the model a real professional. Combine the role with the task, audience and desired result so the instruction has practical meaning. Treat a role as a way to shape the response, not as a credential or guarantee. For a consequential topic, describe the perspective you want and still consult an appropriate human or authoritative source.
Try it: Try: 'Act as a patient tutor. Explain this concept to a beginner, then ask me two questions to check my understanding.'
LESSON 3

Set clear constraints

Tell AI what must be included, what must be avoided, the length, audience and format. Constraints reduce vague results.

In depth: Constraints reduce ambiguity. State limits such as length, time, budget, tone, language or required elements. When a constraint is important, make it explicit instead of expecting AI to infer it from the situation. Separate essential constraints from preferences and make the essential ones easy to check. If instructions conflict, tell AI which requirement takes priority rather than expecting it to resolve the conflict correctly.
Try it: Try: 'Write this in 150 words, for a beginner, with three examples and no technical jargon.'
LESSON 4

Use examples

Showing AI an example of the kind of result you want can be more useful than adding lots of abstract instructions.

In depth: A concrete example can communicate structure or style more precisely than abstract instructions. Give a good example and explain what characteristics matter. Examples are particularly powerful when you are creating something repeatedly. A short, representative sample is usually clearer than a large collection of inconsistent examples. Remove private information and explicitly identify the pattern to follow, such as structure, level of detail or tone.
Try it: Give one good example and say which features should be preserved.
LESSON 5

Ask for alternatives

Instead of accepting the first answer, ask for different approaches and compare their advantages and disadvantages.

In depth: Alternatives are valuable when several reasonable solutions exist. Tell AI which dimension should change—cost, speed, simplicity, tone or creativity—so the options become meaningfully different rather than minor variations of the same answer. Ask for options that differ on a clear dimension and request the main trade-off for each. Then decide which criteria matter in your situation; a list of alternatives does not make the choice for you.
Try it: Try: 'Give me three versions: simplest, most detailed and most practical. Explain when each is best.'
LESSON 6

Ask AI to critique its draft

A second pass can expose unclear sections, missing assumptions or weak arguments. Treat the critique as a review, not as proof of correctness.

In depth: A critique step separates creation from evaluation. Ask AI to inspect its own draft for missing requirements, unclear wording, contradictions or unsupported claims. Treat the critique as a useful second opinion, not as proof that the final result is correct. Give the critique a checklist based on your requirements. Ask for specific evidence from the draft and separate objective issues from suggestions that depend on taste.
Try it: Ask: 'Critique this draft. List the three biggest weaknesses and suggest specific improvements.'
LESSON 7

Build reusable prompt patterns

When a task repeats, save a prompt structure with placeholders for the changing information.

In depth: A reusable prompt pattern captures instructions that work repeatedly while leaving changing details as placeholders. This turns successful conversations into a small personal library and reduces the effort needed to start familiar tasks. Record the task, stable instructions, placeholders and a simple quality check. Revisit the pattern when your process changes so the saved prompt does not continue producing an outdated result.
Try it: Create a template such as: goal + context + constraints + output format + checks.
LESSON 8

Use conversations strategically

Long conversations can accumulate context, but they can also become confusing. Periodically ask for a concise summary of decisions and open questions.

In depth: Conversation history is useful when previous context remains relevant. Use follow-up messages to refine the result rather than repeating everything. When the subject changes, consider starting fresh so old assumptions do not contaminate the new task. A brief recap can preserve decisions, constraints and unresolved questions without carrying every earlier message forward. Correct the recap before continuing if it omits something important.
Try it: Try: 'Summarise what we have decided so far and list what is still unresolved.'
LESSON 9

Know when to start a new chat

A fresh conversation can be better when the subject changes completely or old context could bias the result.

In depth: A new chat is useful when the old context is no longer relevant or has become confusing. Keep the previous conversation when its history genuinely helps. If you start fresh, carry over only the information needed for the new task. A fresh chat can reduce irrelevant carry-over, but it also loses useful context. Bring across a short, checked summary of the goal and constraints rather than copying a long conversation automatically.
Try it: If you change from planning a holiday to writing a formal complaint, starting a new chat can keep the context clean.
LESSON 10

Final challenge

Choose a real low-risk task and use a staged workflow: define the goal, set constraints, ask for options, critique the chosen approach and produce the final result.

In depth: Choose a task that normally requires several steps. Divide it into stages, define a role and constraints, request alternatives and add a critique step. Run the process once and note which stage gave you the most useful improvement. After completing the task, identify which stage prevented rework and where you still had to intervene. Keep the useful checkpoints and change the process where it created unnecessary steps.
Try it: Finish with your own review. The objective is to control the process rather than simply ask one question.
