MODULE 05 · 10 LESSONS

Get better answers

Move beyond basic prompts. Learn how to control depth, tone, structure and reasoning, break complex tasks into stages and revise results deliberately.

LESSON 1

Be specific about the result

A strong request describes the outcome you want, not just the topic. Topic: 'healthy eating'. Result: 'Create a seven-day dinner plan with five ingredients per meal and a shopping list.'

In depth: Specificity means defining what success looks like. “Make this better” is ambiguous; “make this shorter, clearer and friendly while keeping the meaning” gives the model clear directions. Also say what must remain unchanged when that matters. A practical way to improve a request is to name the audience, the use of the answer and the limits it must respect. These details help distinguish a useful response from one that is merely on topic.
Practical idea: Ask yourself: 'What would a useful answer look like on my screen?' Then describe that result to the AI.
LESSON 2

Break complex tasks into steps

Large tasks often become easier when divided into stages. Ask for an outline before the full answer, or have AI analyse the problem before proposing a solution.

In depth: Large tasks often contain dependencies. Planning before drafting and checking before publishing creates checkpoints where you can correct direction early. Staged work also makes it easier to identify which part failed instead of rebuilding everything. For example, ask for a plan, review the plan, then request one section at a time. If a stage is wrong, you can correct it before that mistake shapes the entire result.
Practical idea: For a presentation: first request the structure, then the key message of each slide, then the speaker notes. You can approve or change each stage.
LESSON 3

Control the level of detail

Tell AI whether you need a quick answer, a beginner explanation or a deep analysis. 'Explain simply' and 'analyse in depth' are very different instructions.

In depth: Detail should match purpose and audience. You can request a beginner explanation, a technical treatment, a short summary or a deep explanation with examples. If an answer is too shallow, ask for a specific missing layer such as mechanisms, exceptions or worked examples. You can also set a stopping point, such as 'give me the main idea first, then wait for my questions'. This keeps the answer manageable and lets you choose where deeper explanation is worth the time.
Practical idea: You can also set a practical limit: 'Give me the answer in under 300 words, then add a section called If you want to go deeper.'
LESSON 4

Control tone and voice

Tone changes how information feels. Specify whether you want formal, friendly, warm, neutral, persuasive, concise or conversational language.

In depth: Tone describes the social character of a text: formal, warm, neutral, cautious or direct. Voice is more personal. AI can help adjust both, but preserving your own voice requires review. Tell it what should change and what should remain characteristic of you. For a more reliable edit, provide a short sample of your writing and name traits to preserve, such as directness or humour. Remove any personal details from the sample that the task does not require.
Practical idea: For personal communication, preserve your voice: 'Keep my wording natural and do not make me sound like a company.' For professional writing, specify the audience and level of formality.
LESSON 5

Ask for the right structure

Structure is a powerful form of control. Request headings, bullets, tables, checklists, timelines or a decision matrix according to the job.

In depth: Structure determines how easily information can be understood and used. Processes work well as numbered steps, comparisons as tables, options as grouped bullets and long explanations as sections. Asking for structure early often saves substantial editing later. Choose a format that supports the next action: a checklist for doing, a comparison table for choosing, or a short explanation for understanding. A format is useful when it makes the answer easier to act on.
Practical idea: A useful prompt is: 'Start with a three-line summary. Then give me the detailed steps. Finish with the three most important risks.'
LESSON 6

Use examples and counterexamples

Examples show what you mean. Counterexamples show what you do not want. Together they reduce ambiguity.

In depth: Examples show the target; counterexamples show what should be avoided. Together they define a clearer boundary than abstract instructions alone. This is particularly useful when teaching, formatting recurring work or communicating a style that is difficult to describe. A useful example should resemble the situation you care about. If it contains several features, say which ones matter; otherwise AI may copy accidental details rather than the intended pattern.
Practical idea: For instance: 'Give me titles that sound informative, not sensational. Good: How to Plan a Weekend Trip. Avoid: The ULTIMATE Secret That Will Change Your Life.'
LESSON 7

Request a review before the final version

For important work, ask AI to inspect a draft for gaps before rewriting it. You can ask it to check clarity, completeness, contradictions and unsupported claims.

In depth: A review step asks AI to inspect a draft against explicit criteria such as clarity, missing information, contradictions, tone and factual claims. This is different from immediately asking for another draft because the review first identifies problems you can decide whether to fix. You can make the review more precise by asking it to quote the sentence or section that needs attention and explain why. Then decide which suggestions fit your goal before requesting a revision.
Practical idea: Prompt: 'First identify weaknesses in this draft. Do not rewrite it yet. Group the issues into clarity, structure and factual claims.' Then decide which changes to make.
LESSON 8

Handle uncertainty honestly

When the answer depends on missing information, tell AI not to guess. Ask it to identify uncertainty and list what needs checking.

In depth: When information is incomplete, ask AI to distinguish facts from assumptions and to mark what needs verification. For factual tasks, an explicit “not enough information” is more useful than a confident completion based on invented details. You can make this concrete by asking the model to list its assumptions and the missing facts that would change the answer. That gives you a clear next step instead of a vague disclaimer.
Practical idea: Try: 'If you are not sure, say so. Separate facts, reasonable assumptions and suggestions.' This makes the output easier to evaluate.
LESSON 9

Create reusable prompt templates

If you repeatedly do the same kind of task, create a template with placeholders. This saves time and makes your results more consistent.

In depth: A prompt template turns a successful interaction into a repeatable process. Use placeholders such as [GOAL], [AUDIENCE], [DATE] and [BUDGET]. Keep the instructions that consistently improve results and remove unnecessary wording. A template should be tested on more than one example. If it only works for one case, revise the placeholders or instructions before relying on it for repeated work.
Practical idea: Template: 'I need help with [TASK]. Audience: [AUDIENCE]. Context: [CONTEXT]. Constraints: [LIMITS]. Desired format: [FORMAT]. Ask me up to three questions if something essential is missing.'
LESSON 10

Final practical challenge

Choose a task that normally produces mediocre AI answers. Write a structured prompt with task, context, constraints, format and tone. Generate a result, review it, then request a revised version based on your critique.

In depth: Choose a task you repeat regularly and build a reusable prompt. Define the result, add variable context, specify constraints and choose an output format. Run it once, inspect the result and improve the template. You are creating a personal tool, not just learning a trick. After the first run, note what you had to clarify and update the template. A reusable prompt improves through use; it is a starting point that still needs judgement for each new situation.
Practical idea: Save the final prompt as your own template. The real skill is not memorising a formula—it is learning to communicate what you need clearly and improve the result deliberately.
Do it now: Apply the technique to one real situation from your own life. Keep the task simple enough to finish in 10–15 minutes.
